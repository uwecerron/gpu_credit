\subsection{RQ1: Where the money goes}
Over the window, institutions minted \USDaiMinted{} of USDai and burned \USDaiBurned{}. Minting was backed by PYUSD, by M\textsuperscript{0}'s wrapped \$M (wM), by USDC and, marginally, by USDT (Table~\ref{tab:usdai}). Not every burn is an exit. Loan disbursements also burn USDai into the borrower's currency at origination, and the escrow redemption in Section~\ref{sec:escrow} is a burn too. Of the USDai still outstanding, most sits in the vault as idle deposit balance rather than in loans (Section~\ref{sec:recon}).

From that pool the Loan Routers originated \NLoans{} loans (excluding \NTestLoans{} test loans below \$1{,}000) for \Originated{} of principal to \NBorrowers{} distinct borrower addresses. \NMigrated{} loans have a recorded migration event and four were refinanced into new terms; root originations are counted once. \NRepaid{} loans have been repaid in full. None has a recorded breach or liquidation event in the observed period; that is not a lifetime default estimate. At \StudyEnd{}, \Outstanding{} was outstanding. Borrowers have paid \InterestPaid{} of interest, \PrincipalRepaid{} of principal and \FeesPaid{} of repayment fees. Fig.~\ref{fig:orig} shows originations by month and borrower.

\subsection{What the collateral is}\label{sec:collateral}
Every loan's collateral is an ERC-721 token from the protocol's hardware contract (``USD.AI Tokenized Hardware''), either directly or wrapped in a bundle. Each minted token's \texttt{tokenURI}, read from the contract, points to a metadata record the protocol publishes with the server model, the number of units, a stated collateral value, a useful life and a depreciation start. We read token IDs from decoded \texttt{LoanTermsV2} and bundle transfer logs, and URIs and owners by \texttt{eth\_call}. Including two additional records served by the protocol's metadata host for token IDs not minted at retrieval, the host returned \CollRecords{} records (retrieved \CollRetrieved{}). The records are the protocol's own statements, hosted off-chain and changeable, so what follows is what USD.AI says the collateral is, not an appraisal. Unminted records are excluded from active pledged collateral.

Table~\ref{tab:collateral} and Fig.~\ref{fig:collateral} list the active loans. The metadata describes NVIDIA B300, B200 and H200 servers and, in the small early loans, RTX 6000 and RTX 5090 workstations. Our aggregate count is \CollUnits{} hardware units and approximately \CollGPUs{} GPUs, with \CollValue{} of stated value against \Outstanding{} outstanding: an indicated book loan-to-value of \BookLTV{}, ranging from \LTVMin{} to \LTVMax{} by loan. The eight-GPU-per-server assumption does not explain every mixed or aggregated row in Table~\ref{tab:collateral}; those records need a model-by-model reconciliation before treating the aggregate as an independently verified fleet count. Every record states a useful life of \UsefulLife{} days, about three years, with depreciation starting ``on borrow''. Under straight-line depreciation and no principal reduction, an 80\% initial LTV would reach 100\% after roughly 7.2 months. The date changes with amortization, market resale values and any contractual credit protection, which are not established by the metadata~\cite{cerron2026gpu}.

One token stands out. \UnpledgedName{}, \UnpledgedUnits{} servers with a stated value of \UnpledgedValue{}, secured the largest borrower's \UnpledgedPrior{} loan until that loan was repaid in full on 2026-10-01, eight days after the \EscrowOpen{} escrow deposits. The token now sits unpledged in the borrower's reserve account. The host also serves records for two unminted GB200 NVL72 token IDs, 1012 and 1013, with \UnmintedValue{} in combined stated value. A refinancing in progress is one possible explanation for this sequence, but no matching new loan or legal commitment can be verified from these observations. Neither unminted record proves the existence of pledged collateral.

\subsection{Maturities and payment status}
The \NActive{} active loans have original terms of \TermMin{} to \TermMax{} years. Weighted by outstanding principal, the book has \YearsToMaturity{} years left to run: \MatTwentyEight{} matures in 2028 and \MatTwentyNine{} in 2029. Against that, completed redemptions have a 13-day median wait (Section~\ref{sec:queue}). The longest gap since a loan's last recorded repayment is \MaxDaysSincePayment{} days, inside the reported monthly schedule, and \NLate{} loans are flagged late by the event-based test. The oldest active loan is under a year old. The absence of observed delinquency is therefore evidence about a young, right-censored loan book, not its full-cycle credit performance.

\subsection{RQ2: Where the yield comes from}
The deposit-side NAV of sUSDai rose from \NavStart{} to \NavEnd{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:nav}). That is \AnnTotal{} annualized over the full window and \AnnNinety{} over the last 90 days. Multiplying daily NAV changes by the outstanding share supply gives \Accrued{} of yield credited to holders.

Against that, the vault recorded \CashInterest{} of loan interest, \CashBase{} of harvested base yield (net of a \AdminFee{} admin fee), \CashFees{} of origination and repayment fees, and \CashEscrow{} of escrow interest (net of the router admin fee). The event-based cash-to-NAV-growth ratio is $\kappa=$\,\CashCoverage{}. Counting only borrower interest and fees, the ratio is \KappaBorrowers{}. Neither measure is a borrower debt-service coverage ratio, and the timing of recognition and receipt can differ. Fig.~\ref{fig:yield} shows the monthly split. Base yield was the largest recorded historical source, at \CashBase{} against \CashInterest{} of borrower interest. \CashBasePYUSD{} came from PYUSD issuer incentives harvested out of the Base Yield Escrow (a \texttt{USDai.Harvested} event in the same transaction). \CashBaseWM{} was minted from M\textsuperscript{0}'s wM, the yield-bearing stablecoin that backed USDai early on, including a single \$7.82\,M deposit on 2026-02-02. The latter one-off event materially affects the full-window comparison.

Table~\ref{tab:kappa} separates recurring receipts from two exceptional events. The first is the \$7.82\,M wM deposit. The second is an October 2, 2026 transaction in which an escrow holding \$1{,}000 for roughly two hours generated approximately \$379\,k in gross contract-booked ``escrow interest'', of which approximately \EscrowAnomalous{} reached the vault after fees. This is not evidence of market-rate interest earned by an ordinary loan. The chain establishes the amounts and their contract classification but cannot determine the transfer's economic purpose. Both exceptional receipts are included in the historical \CashCoverage{} coverage ratio and are isolated in the table; excluding them from the numerator is a composition sensitivity, not a forecast of recurring yield. The results establish the historical composition of recognized vault income, not the recurring yield mix after reserve assets and incentive programs change.

Interest also goes to lenders other than the vault. Of the interest paid, \InterestToVault{} (\VaultInterestShare{}) went to sUSDai. \InterestToOthers{} went to two other tranche lenders: a junior tranche priced at 28\% APR and a one-unit senior position that collects repayment fees. Loans are almost entirely vault-funded (\SusdaiFunded{} of current principal).

\subsection{Reconciliation with vault state}\label{sec:recon}
At block \StateBlock{} the vault reports \TotalAssets{} of assets against \TotalShares{} shares (Fig.~\ref{fig:bs}). Shares split into \HubSupply{} on Arbitrum, \BridgedSupply{} bridged out and a residual of \PendingRedeemShares{}, which the contract counts as pending redemption. The event scan and state call now use that same block. Our own redemption queue shows \PendingShares{} pending. Event-derived supply plus that queue differs from \texttt{totalShares} by approximately $6\times10^{-8}$ shares, negligible at the displayed precision. This exact-block identity is a check on share accounting, not independent verification of cross-chain holder balances. The bridged supply is close to the sum of the three EVM spoke snapshots plus Solana (\SpokeSolana{}). The vault's \texttt{pendingLoanBalance} is \PendingLoanBalance{}. Our stitched loan book exceeds it by \LoanBookGap{} (\LoanBookGapPct{}). Although the loan accounting includes accrued interest, the available fields do not yet provide an itemized bridge explaining the \$393\,k residual. This is a useful consistency check, not proof that every reconstructed loan or off-chain claim is correct.

The assets side breaks down as \DepositBalance{} of idle deposit balance, \PendingLoanBalance{} of active loans, \AccruedLoanInterest{} of accrued loan interest, \BaseYieldAccrued{} of unharvested base yield, \EscrowAccrued{} of accrued escrow interest and \DTBalance{} of \texttt{depositTimelockBalance}. That last item is \DTShare{} of assets, and it is not a loan.

\subsection{Checking GoldRush against the chain}\label{sec:verify}
Almost every number in this paper comes from GoldRush, so we pulled the underlying logs again from a public Arbitrum node with \texttt{eth\_getLogs}, without GoldRush, over the same block range. For each of the \NVerify{} event sets the analysis depends on, we compare the two sources log by log, identifying each log by its transaction hash, topics and data, and we compare the sum of the key amount (Table~\ref{tab:verify}). \NVerifyMatch{} of \NVerify{} sets are identical, covering \NVerifyLogs{} logs.

The check found one real problem. GoldRush's event endpoint treats \texttt{ending-block} as exclusive, so our first scan skipped the last block of every 1{,}000{,}000-block window. We confirmed this directly: a log in block $B$ is returned for the range $[B, B{+}1]$ but not for $[B, B]$. We fixed the request, fetched the 182 window-end blocks for every scanned event and re-ran the check. The patch added two Livepeer logs (one ticket payment of 0.0024 ETH) and nothing to the USD.AI events. We also found that, for some transactions, GoldRush numbers logs differently from the node, so we never match or deduplicate logs on their index.

For token supply, \texttt{totalSupply} at the snapshot block equals the sum of GoldRush holder balances exactly on Base and is \SupplyGapPlasma{} higher on Plasma. The public Arbitrum and Ethereum nodes do not serve historical state, so their latest-block comparisons use a different timestamp and differ from the holder snapshots by approximately \SupplyGapArbitrum{} and $-$\SupplyGapEth{}, respectively. These differences should not be conflated with the negligible same-block share-accounting residual above. The escrow redemption receipt, re-read from the node, shows the same burn of 128{,}895{,}244.1 USDai and the same PYUSD payment to the same wallet. At the later block \BackingBlock{}, the USDai contract held \USDaiReserves{} of stablecoins (\USDaiPYUSD{} of it PYUSD) against \USDaiSupplyNow{} of USDai counted across the hub and EVM spokes, a measured coverage of \USDaiCoverage{} for that scope and instant. This observation does not by itself prove redemption availability under stress, recovery of sUSDai credit claims, or an omnichain reserve audit.

\subsection{Escrow pre-funding}\label{sec:escrow}
The vault made \NEscrow{} escrow timelock deposits totaling \EscrowDeposits{}. Most closed normally: \NEscrowClosed{} were either withdrawn into an originated loan or cancelled (\NEscrowCancelled{} cancellations, which returned principal plus interest to the vault). \NEscrowOpen{}, both made on 2026-09-23 and worth \EscrowOpen{}, carry a \texttt{loanTermsHash} that no Loan Router has originated and that has not been cancelled. Together they equal \texttt{depositTimelockBalance} to the cent. They earn an escrow rate of \EscrowAPR{}, which the vault accrues optimistically. None of the timelock contracts holds any tokens: \texttt{balanceOf} returns zero for USDai, PYUSD and USDC.

Fig.~\ref{fig:flow} and Table~\ref{tab:escrow} show the traced transfers. Every escrow deposit moves USDai from the vault to a single address we call the escrow facility (\EscrowDeposits{} in total, of which cancellations returned part). It is a Gnosis Safe, and its transactions are submitted by accounts also observed submitting the protocol's multisig transactions. This suggests operational overlap, not verified common legal ownership. For the two open deposits, the facility redeemed USDai into PYUSD at the USDai contract on the same day and paid PYUSD to a wallet we call the PYUSD hub. The hub was first active on 2026-04-29 and has 56 transactions. It also received PYUSD from the largest borrower and from an account that submits the protocol's multisig transactions. It transferred large amounts to a high-activity destination labeled ``off-ramp~A'' in the figure, which holds PYUSD and USDG and is active on 19 chains, and smaller amounts to another service-like wallet. Neither label identifies a verified custodian or ultimate beneficiary. The fee recipient also routes PYUSD to the high-activity destination.

Escrow pre-funding is contemplated by the contracts and may correspond to a signed facility not yet originated on-chain. At the observed snapshot, \DTBalance{} of reported sUSDai assets represents two accounting claims with no corresponding originated loan or wrapped GPU collateral in the analyzed contracts. The tokens were redeemed and transferred through external wallets. Because PYUSD is fungible, following gross transfers through a commingled wallet does not establish ownership or the destination of each deposited dollar. The chain alone cannot determine whether the vault has segregated funds, contractual repayment rights, or other off-chain security. Optimistic escrow yield is accrued before a matching on-chain loan exists; legal status, recoverability and subsequent settlement require independent verification and a response from the protocol (Section~\ref{sec:disc}).

On every chain, a handful of contracts and pools custody much of the supply (Fig.~\ref{fig:conc}). The largest identified DeFi venue is Pendle, which holds \PendleArb{} of Arbitrum sUSDai. On Plasma, \PendlePlasmaUSDai{} of USDai sits in Pendle and another \VaultPlasmaUSDai{} in vault strategies.

\subsection{RQ5: Redemptions and GPU prices}\label{sec:queue}
Holders filed \NRedeemRequests{} redemption requests from \NRedeemers{} addresses, for \RequestedShares{} shares. Among completed requests, the median wait was \WaitMedian{} days, the 90th percentile \WaitPNinety{} days, and the longest \WaitMax{} days. Another \PendingShares{} shares remained pending at the cutoff; their final wait times are right-censored. These completed-request statistics do not measure the unconditional exit-time distribution or dollar-weighted experience. Servicing is lumpy (Fig.~\ref{fig:queue}), with large batches: three calls on 2026-03-07 and 2026-04-06 together paid out about \$217\,M. Priced in PYUSD, which reduces feed noise common to both tokens, USDai never traded below \USDaiRatioMin{} (on \USDaiRatioMinDate{}). Selling on a DEX provides another route to exit, subject to market depth and price.

The available weekly series does not identify a causal effect of GPU prices on redemptions. Over \GPUWeeks{} overlapping weeks, Spearman's $\rho$ between weekly requested shares and the H100 rental price is \RhoLevel{} in levels and \RhoChange{} in weekly changes (Fig.~\ref{fig:redgpu}). Protocol growth and declining rental-price trends confound the level correlation. The near-zero change correlation is descriptive only; it neither establishes independence nor rules out lagged responses, benchmark mismatch or effects conditional on contractual revenue exposure.

\subsection{RQ7: GPU credit and observable compute payments}
Over the same window, Livepeer gateways paid orchestrators \LPETH{} ETH (\LPUSD{}) in \LPTickets{} winning tickets. \LPGateways{} gateways paid \LPOrchestrators{} orchestrators. The largest gateway accounts for \LPTopGateway{} of payments and the top three for \LPTopThree{} (HHI \LPHHIG{}). On the supply side, payments are spread out (HHI \LPHHIO{}). Livepeer's payments mix video transcoding and AI inference, and the events do not separate the two.

Akash, measured through its own API, recorded \AKUSD{} of spend in the window and \AKAllTime{} all-time. Active leased GPUs ranged from \AKGPUMin{} to \AKGPUMax{}. On \AKRetrieved{}, the network reported \AKGPUNow{} of \AKGPUTotal{} GPUs active across \AKProviders{} providers (Fig.~\ref{fig:akash}). The median Akash H100 price was \AKHundred{}/hr and the A100 \AKAHundred{}/hr, against a centralized-cloud H100 median of \CentralH{}/hr (Fig.~\ref{fig:prices}).

Fig.~\ref{fig:markets} and Table~\ref{tab:markets} put the three observed flows side by side on a log scale. USD.AI's gross loan originations are \CreditMultiple{} times the \ComputeSpend{} of measured Akash and Livepeer payments over the window. This ratio compares capital advanced with operating payments, and the borrower populations need not overlap. It cannot be interpreted as a ratio of credit to underlying compute demand, nor does the sample span all decentralized or private compute transactions. USD.AI borrower-address concentration (HHI \HHIOrig{}) and Livepeer gateway concentration (HHI \LPHHIG{}) describe different participant populations (Fig.~\ref{fig:lmk}). The compute use financed by the reconstructed loans is not independently linked to on-chain rental or inference receipts in this dataset.

\subsection{What the debt implies about the compute}\label{sec:implied}
The chain records principal, contractual rates and maturities, but not actual GPU rental revenue. The current outstanding balance implies approximately \DebtInterest{} of annual interest at the stated rates. If, as an illustrative assumption rather than a recovered contractual payment schedule, principal amortizes straight-line to maturity, annual principal payments would be \DebtAmort{} and total modeled debt service \DebtService{}. Our earlier work treated machine labor as hours of work rather than units of hardware~\cite{liquidlabor,cerron2026gpu}; that frame permits a transparent sensitivity calculation. At the Akash H100 median of \AKHundred{} per hour, \DebtService{} would require \GPUhAkashPrice{}\,M billable GPU-hours per year before operating expenses. At 70\% utilization, this is the equivalent of \GPUsAkashPrice{} continuously available H100s and \MWAkashPrice{}\,MW at an assumed 1.5\,kW per GPU before cooling. At the centralized-cloud H100 price of \CentralH{}, the equivalent is \GPUsCentralPrice{} GPUs (\MWCentralPrice{}\,MW); at a hypothetical \$1.50 per hour, it is \GPUsLowPrice{} (\MWLowPrice{}\,MW). Fig.~\ref{fig:implied} plots this H100-equivalent sensitivity, not the actual required B200/B300 deployment. The pledged hardware is model-heterogeneous and its realized prices, utilization, customer terms, power cost and scheduled principal payments are not observed. Dividing the modeled debt service by \CollGPUs{} GPUs at 70\% utilization yields \Breakeven{} per GPU-hour (\BreakevenFull{} at full utilization), before any operating expense. This is a conditional gross-revenue hurdle, not evidence that the fleet can service debt.

The hypothetical threshold can be tested only with independently verified unit counts, contractual repayment schedules, utilization, realized receipts, operating costs, customer commitments and collateral rights. Publication of a hardware-token record alone is insufficient for that underwriting test.

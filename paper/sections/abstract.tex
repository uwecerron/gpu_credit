\begin{abstract}
Several synthetic-dollar protocols take stablecoin deposits and lend them to companies that operate GPU clusters. The GPUs, the borrowers and the loan contracts are off-chain, but the debt is recorded on a public chain. We rebuild one such protocol, USD.AI on Arbitrum, from \EventsDecoded{} decoded events between \StudyStart{} and \StudyEnd{}, using the Covalent GoldRush API and event signatures taken from the protocol's source code. We find \NLoans{} loans to \NBorrowers{} borrowers, with \Originated{} originated and \Outstanding{} outstanding, rates between \RateMin{} and \RateMax{}, and no defaults. The largest borrower received \TopOneShare{} of all lending (HHI \HHIOrig{}). The protocol's own hardware-token metadata puts about \CollGPUs{} current-generation GPUs behind the active loans at a \BookLTV{} loan-to-value on a three-year life. The sUSDai vault grew \AnnTotal{} a year and \CashCoverage{} of that yield has arrived as cash, but more of the cash came from stablecoin issuers (M\textsuperscript{0} and PYUSD) than from borrower interest. The median redemption waited \WaitMedian{} days, and redemptions do not move with GPU rental prices. Checked against the vault's own accounting at one block, our loan book is within \LoanBookGapPct{} and our share count within \SharesGapPct{}. The same check shows that \DTShare{} of reported assets is escrow for loans that do not exist on-chain. The escrowed funds were redeemed to PYUSD and sent to an off-ramp wallet. Over the same period, Akash and Livepeer, two established decentralized compute markets, took in \ComputeSpend{}, about \CreditMultiple{} times less than the vault lent. The code and a hash-verified dataset reproduce every number.
\end{abstract}
